# Product hypothesis

## Vision

Imagine a workshop on your property that looks after the machines and objects you love. Leave a bicycle with a problem, an old motorbike, a boat component, a 1950s Kenwood mixer or a fridge that needs restoration. A team of software, sensors, tools, specialist people and eventually robots knows the history, investigates what is wrong, finds or makes an appropriate repair plan, carries out authorised work and verifies it. One day, some jobs happen overnight and you wake to a repaired object and an account of the work.

The ambition: you should not have to replace something simply because it is old, mysterious or difficult to repair. Repair and upgrades can extend useful life and may reduce environmental impact; the right choice depends on the item, repair, energy use and alternatives. Some items cannot be repaired economically or safely.

## First user and machine

Colm, as owner or prospective buyer of a 1996 VW T4 2.5 TDI. Ownership, engine, ECU and diagnostic access are unconfirmed. Start with real information and clearly mark unknowns. A simple household object available to Colm can be a parallel candidate for a first physical repair.

## First product hypothesis

Given a genuine service history and at least one real observation, a persistent caretaker can help the owner understand what is known, what is unknown, and what to do next, with a brief a mechanic or repairer can use. Record the result and learn whether it helped. Preventing breakdowns and predicting failures are unproven.

## Long-term hypotheses

- An individual machine's history, repeated observations and expert input can help make useful repair decisions.
- Cameras, thermal, sound, vibration, pressure, electrical and existing machine data can provide complementary evidence, subject to fit and validation.
- Increasingly capable physical tools and robots can perform narrowly defined tasks, then longer supervised workflows, and eventually suitable unattended work in a guarded workshop.
- People may value an enduring care service, including repairs and restoration, enough to pay for it.

These are hypotheses and ambitions, not existing capabilities.

## Buyer hypothesis

T4 owners or independent specialists might pay for reliable ongoing care and a usable maintenance history. Later candidates include owners of treasured appliances, bicycles and boats, and repair workshops. Test with a specific person, a real case and a paid-pilot ask. Open-source software and a future paid service can coexist; packaging and pricing need validation.

## Evidence and boundaries

Each observation has a timestamp, source, units where applicable and context. Separate raw evidence, interpretation, plausible explanations, assumptions and verified outcomes. A missing reading does not mean healthy. A proposed repair needs a tested outcome and appropriate independent inspection. The owner controls private machine records. Purchases and outward communications require authorisation.

Physical work must be bounded to the specific object, task, tools and workshop, with safety controls, failure recovery and appropriate expert involvement. Automated road-safety, electrical, pressure and refrigerant work requires specific qualified assessment rather than being assumed safe.

## First experiment non-goals

Universal prediction, ECU programming, autonomous driving, unattended robot repair, remote machine actuation and claims of detecting all faults.
