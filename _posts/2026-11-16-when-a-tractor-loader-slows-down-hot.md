Say the loader on an old tractor works fine when you start the morning. An hour into moving bales, the same job takes longer. By the time a mechanic arrives, the machine has cooled down and the fault is hard to reproduce. An owner of a John Deere 2355 describes much the same pattern in a public repair thread. Their account gives us a symptom and the conditions under which it appeared. The cause needs its own test. [John Deere 2355 owner discussion](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/john-deere-2355-hydraulic-problems.593252/)

## First, record what the tractor was doing

Paolo Lindia, Riccardo Cantini, Francesco Bettucci, Luigi Sartori and Paolo Trunfio studied how operating context changes what counts as an unusual tractor reading. Their paper, published online for the December 2026 issue of *Computers in Industry*, uses two work sessions from a New Holland T7.165 S during tillage at the University of Padova’s experimental farm. The research team collected position and CAN bus data once a second and labelled when the tractor was working, turning, moving or idling. [Research paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compind.2026.104539) · [University publication record](https://scalab.dimes.unical.it/publications/) · [Dataset and methods](https://github.com/lindiap/tractor-field-operations-dataset)

The field recordings are real. The test faults in the evaluation were added to those recordings using rules developed with language models. The result is a way to test whether the software notices a planned disturbance amid real changes in activity. It leaves open how well it would spot wear on a working farm. A separate hydraulic test rig published through UCI measured pressure, flow and temperature while researchers deliberately varied component condition. That gives researchers a source of physically measured examples under controlled conditions. [Tractor dataset and synthetic-fault workflow](https://github.com/lindiap/tractor-field-operations-dataset) · [UCI hydraulic test-rig dataset](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/447/condition+monitoring+of+hydraulic+systems)

## Try it on the tractor you already have

An older loader may have no useful data socket. For a first trial, have the operator and mechanic agree a normal, repeatable task with a clear work area. From a safe position outside the working zone, record a few comparable cycles when cold and when the symptom appears. Keep the attachment, load and engine speed as consistent as practicable. Log the cycle time from video, the operating conditions and any temperature the machine already shows. An external surface reading can also help mark the warm-up. Record the location and method and label it as surface temperature.

That record can show **when the loader slows down and whether it does so again**. If the finding warrants a hydraulic test, the mechanic can measure flow, pressure and fluid temperature under representative conditions against the machine’s service information. Let the mechanic handle any connection to the hydraulic circuit. Keep people clear of moving equipment and follow the machine’s isolation and support procedures before maintenance. [Webtec’s field-testing guide](https://en.webtec.com/education/faqs/DHM-Digital-Hydraulic-Tester/) · [Ireland’s Health and Safety Authority](https://www.hsa.ie/eng/your_industry/agriculture_forestry/vehicles_machinery/machinery/)

In this trial the operator stays in the cab and the software records. Assisted guidance gives a driver information about the job. Remote operation moves the human operator’s controls off the machine. Autonomous operation gives software control of a defined task. Turning a loader into a remotely operated or autonomous machine would be a separate project, with an assessed work zone and tests of how it stops if communications or sensing fail.

## Would it help the next callout?

Ireland’s CSO estimated **201,260 tractors owned on farms in 2023**. Its published machinery breakdown is by power, so that figure cannot tell us how many are older loader tractors or what their owners would pay. Mobile diagnosis is already an alternative. A Waterford agricultural and plant repair company advertises on-site diagnostics, and *Irish Tractor* has documented a Kerry mobile repairer carrying flow meters for pump tests. [CSO farm machinery figures](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-fss/farmstructuresurvey2023/farmmachineryandrenewableenergy/) · [Waterford mobile repair service](https://www.bdagriandplant.ie/mobile-plant--agricultural-mechanic) · [Irish Tractor’s reporting](https://irishtractor.ie/professional-on-site-repairs-for-tractors-machinery-and-plant/)

The first potential buyer is a farmer already paying for callouts on a loader with a recurring warm fault. Try one case with their usual mechanic. Ask the mechanic what they would check from the owner’s description alone, then show them the record. At the end of the job, see whether the extra evidence changed the first test or saved a return visit. Record the time spent collecting it, the eventual finding and whether the same job runs better after the repair. A paid follow-on trial makes sense if the mechanic and owner can point to time or a repeat visit saved.

**Have a loader that behaves differently after an hour?** Share the make, model, attachment, a short cold and warm comparison and what the mechanic eventually found. Share a failed reproduction too. Keep farm locations and machine serial numbers out of public logs.
